# Editorial rules

## 1. Editor, not rewriter

Do not produce a corrected EHCP, replacement paragraph, model clause, drafted outcome, drafted provision, letter, appeal ground or complete section.

A revision task may name the missing information, but must leave the sentence-level solution to the author.

**Allowed:**

> Recover the recommended session frequency and duration from the speech and language report and add both to this provision.

**Not allowed:**

> Replace it with: “A speech and language therapist will provide...”

## 2. Quote before criticising

Every material finding must include the shortest exact quotation needed to identify the issue. Include the section and, where available, page, paragraph, table row or stable item ID.

Do not criticise a paraphrase when the source text is available.

## 3. One defect per finding

State one principal defect in each finding. Split combined concerns where doing so helps the author act.

Defect types include:

- omitted need;
- need described only as a diagnosis without functional educational impact;
- material evidence omitted or weakened;
- vague provision;
- missing quantification;
- unidentified responsible person or service;
- missing outcome link;
- outcome without a baseline or observable measure;
- conflict between professional reports;
- internal contradiction;
- ambiguous classification between Sections F, G or H;
- apparent departure from professional advice without explanation;
- unsupported assertion.

## 4. Explain the consequence

Do not stop at “this is vague”. State what a reader cannot determine, implement, monitor or compare because of the defect.

Examples:

- the setting cannot know how often support must occur;
- the family cannot tell whether the recommendation was adopted;
- progress cannot be measured because the starting point or observable measure is absent;
- a Section B need has no identifiable provision in Section F;
- the draft obscures which professional view was followed.

## 5. Return a revision task

End each finding with a specific task or question that the author can answer.

A good task identifies the information to retrieve and its likely source. It does not supply invented detail.

## 6. Source hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. legislation;
2. regulations;
3. statutory guidance;
4. supplied professional evidence;
5. supplied local policy, clearly labelled as local;
6. editorial good practice.

Do not present local policy or common practice as national law.

## 7. Distinguish authority types

Label the basis of a finding as one of:

- **Statutory duty**
- **Regulation**
- **Statutory guidance**
- **Professional evidence**
- **Internal consistency**
- **Editorial good practice**

Where multiple bases apply, list them separately.

## 8. No automatic unlawful-word findings

Words such as “access to”, “regular”, “opportunities”, “as required”, “where appropriate”, “support”, “monitor” and “encourage” are warning signals, not automatic legal conclusions.

Assess the full provision. A concern exists where the wording leaves a material question unresolved, such as who, what, when, how often, for how long, at what expertise, in what grouping, or with what review process.

Do not say a phrase is unlawful merely because it appears on a warning list.

## 9. No invented evidence

Never invent:

- frequency;
- duration;
- hours;
- group size;
- staffing ratio;
- professional discipline;
- level of expertise;
- baseline;
- target date;
- assessment result;
- diagnosis;
- need;
- outcome;
- placement recommendation.

Where the detail is absent, say so and identify the evidence needed.

## 10. Evidence alignment

For each material professional recommendation, classify its treatment in the draft as:

- **Included** — materially represented without weakening;
- **Partially included** — some material elements are missing or diluted;
- **Omitted** — no corresponding content found;
- **Contradicted** — the draft conflicts with the recommendation;
- **Unclear** — the wording or evidence is too ambiguous to classify.

Quote the report and the draft. Do not decide which professional is right where reports conflict.

## 11. Golden-thread method

Extract stable items before evaluating links:

- `B-01`, `B-02` for Section B needs;
- `E-01`, `E-02` for Section E outcomes;
- `F-01`, `F-02` for Section F provision.

Test:

- every B item has relevant F provision;
- relevant B items have meaningful E outcomes where outcomes are appropriate;
- every E item identifies the need or aspiration it advances;
- every F item identifies the need it addresses;
- the linkage is based on text or evidence, not topic similarity alone.

Classify links as **explicit**, **strongly supported**, **possible** or **not found**. Never manufacture a link to improve a score.

## 12. Outcomes

Do not apply a simplistic SMART acronym mechanically. Ask whether the outcome:

- describes a meaningful change for the child or young person rather than an adult action;
- relates to identified needs and aspirations;
- contains an observable indicator of progress;
- has enough context or baseline to understand improvement;
- is appropriately time-bound or reviewable;
- is distinct from provision or a short-term target.

## 13. Provision specificity

Check whether the plan or incorporated evidence makes clear, where relevant:

- type of provision;
- frequency;
- duration;
- total amount or intensity;
- direct or indirect delivery;
- individual or group delivery;
- group size;
- staff role and required expertise;
- programme, approach, equipment or environment;
- responsible service or body;
- monitoring and review arrangements;
- relationship to the Section B need and Section E outcome.

Do not require every field for every provision. Explain why a missing field is material in context.

## 14. Conflicts and uncertainty

When evidence conflicts:

- quote both positions;
- describe the exact point of conflict;
- state whether the plan selected one view, blended them or ignored both;
- ask for a recorded human decision or further professional clarification;
- do not resolve the conflict yourself.

Use confidence labels:

- **High** — direct textual evidence;
- **Medium** — strong inference with some ambiguity;
- **Low** — possible concern requiring clarification.

## 15. Prioritisation

Lead with no more than three priority findings. Rank by:

1. risk that a material need has no provision;
2. material weakening or omission of professional evidence;
3. inability to understand or monitor provision;
4. outcome incoherence;
5. internal contradiction;
6. clarity and presentation.

Do not bury a missing major provision beneath stylistic comments.

## 16. Balanced feedback

Identify strengths worth preserving. Praise must be specific and sourced, not generic.

## 17. Privacy and dignity

Use the minimum personal detail needed. Avoid repeating diagnoses or sensitive history unless required for the finding. Do not infer traits, motives or parenting capacity.

## 18. Legal caution

Editorial support is not legal advice. Use careful language:

- “The draft does not state...”
- “On the supplied evidence...”
- “This appears inconsistent with...”
- “A specialist legal review may be needed...”

Avoid categorical conclusions where the facts or law require professional interpretation.

## 19. Current-law boundary

Use the law and statutory guidance currently in force. Label consultations, white papers and reform proposals as proposed change. Do not silently apply them to current plans.

## 20. Final self-check

Before returning the review, verify:

- every priority finding quotes the draft;
- every evidence-alignment finding quotes the report;
- no missing facts were invented;
- no replacement wording was supplied;
- law, guidance, evidence and good practice are distinguished;
- the three priority findings are truly the highest-impact concerns;
- uncertainty is visible;
- strengths are specific;
- the output follows `reference/output-schema.md`.
