# Examples

All examples are anonymised and illustrative.

## Example 1: vague provision

### Weak critique

> Consider making the speech and language provision more specific.

### Strong editor response

**Finding ID:** P1  
**Location:** Section F, paragraph 3  
**Exact passage:** “The pupil will have regular access to speech and language support.”  
**Defect:** The provision does not state the delivery model, frequency, duration, responsible practitioner or review arrangement.  
**Why it matters:** A reader cannot determine what must be delivered or compare delivery against the plan.  
**Basis:** Statutory guidance and internal clarity.  
**Revision task:** Return to the speech and language evidence and add the recommended delivery model, frequency, duration, practitioner expertise and review arrangements. Where the report does not contain these details, identify the gap in the advice rather than selecting figures.  
**Severity:** Critical  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 2: need without provision

**Finding ID:** P1  
**Location:** Section B, item B-04 and Section F review  
**Exact passage:** “She becomes overwhelmed by unpredictable transitions and may leave the learning space.”  
**Defect:** No Section F item was found that clearly addresses transition preparation or the educational impact described in B-04.  
**Why it matters:** The draft identifies a material special educational need but gives the setting no corresponding provision to meet it.  
**Basis:** Golden-thread review and statutory guidance.  
**Revision task:** Identify the professional recommendation addressing transitions and either link the existing Section F item explicitly to B-04 or add the omitted provision through the normal drafting process. Do not infer a programme that is not in the evidence.  
**Severity:** Critical  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 3: diagnosis without functional need

**Exact passage:** “Autism Spectrum Disorder.”  
**Defect:** This records a diagnosis but does not explain the associated learning difficulty, functional impact or educational need for this child.  
**Why it matters:** Section F provision cannot be reliably matched to a diagnostic label alone.  
**Basis:** Professional evidence and editorial clarity.  
**Revision task:** Extract the individual educational impacts described in the assessment reports and record each material need separately. Do not generalise from the diagnosis.  
**Severity:** Major  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 4: outcome is actually provision

**Exact passage:** “By July, he will receive two occupational therapy sessions each week.”  
**Defect:** This describes an adult or service action rather than the change sought for the child or young person.  
**Why it matters:** Progress cannot be evaluated because the statement measures delivery, not the intended difference in participation, skill or independence.  
**Basis:** Statutory guidance and editorial methodology.  
**Revision task:** Identify the functional change the therapy is intended to achieve and the observable indicator of progress. Keep the therapy frequency in the relevant provision section.  
**Severity:** Major  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 5: material weakening of professional advice

**Professional report:** “A qualified teacher of the deaf should provide direct individual teaching for 45 minutes each week and review the programme termly.”  
**Draft passage:** “Access to specialist hearing support when required.”  
**Defect:** The draft removes direct delivery, individual format, weekly frequency, duration, practitioner qualification and termly review.  
**Why it matters:** The draft is materially weaker than the supplied recommendation and does not show whether the recommendation was rejected or altered intentionally.  
**Basis:** Professional evidence and statutory guidance.  
**Revision task:** Record whether the recommendation is accepted. If it is, restore each material delivery element. If it is not, document the decision-maker's reasons and the evidence relied upon.  
**Severity:** Critical  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 6: conflicting reports

**Report A:** “The pupil requires an individual low-arousal workspace throughout literacy lessons.”  
**Report B:** “The pupil should remain within the class group with environmental adjustments and a planned regulation break.”  
**Draft passage:** “A suitable learning environment will be provided.”  
**Defect:** The professional evidence conflicts on the delivery environment, while the draft obscures rather than resolves the disagreement.  
**Why it matters:** Staff cannot tell which approach has been selected, and the rationale for departing from either recommendation is not visible.  
**Basis:** Professional evidence.  
**Revision task:** Obtain or record a multi-professional decision on the intended environment, its triggers and review criteria. State which recommendation was followed and why.  
**Severity:** Major  
**Confidence:** High

## Example 7: possible concern, not automatic failure

**Exact passage:** “Access to a quiet room for regulation.”  
**Context:** A preceding sentence states the room is available immediately on the pupil's agreed communication signal, identifies the room, names the responsible staff and requires a return-to-learning check.  
**Editorial judgment:** Do not flag the words “access to” alone. The surrounding provision answers the material delivery questions. Review only whether frequency limits, supervision or availability remain unclear in the full evidence.  
**Confidence:** Medium

## Example 8: unacceptable rewriting

### Do not output

> Replace the outcome with: “By July 2027, X will independently use a visual schedule in four out of five transitions...”

### Output instead

> The outcome lacks a baseline, context and observable measure. Ask the author to identify the current level of independence, the transitions covered, the method of observation and the review point. Do not select a percentage or target date unless supported by the evidence.

## Example 9: specific strength

**Exact passage:** “A trained teaching assistant will deliver the named phonological programme individually for 20 minutes, four times each week, with half-termly review by the specialist teacher.”  
**Strength:** This provision identifies the programme, delivery format, duration, frequency, staff role and review responsibility. Preserve those delivery elements when revising adjacent wording.  
**Basis:** Editorial good practice and statutory guidance.
